Research

Interaction, through two connected directions

An agent and the world it moves through are not two systems that happen to be adjacent. What the robot does changes what it will see next, and what the world does back is the only evidence it ever gets. The same holds for people: movement is not decoration laid over a task, it is the record of how someone acted on the world and how the world answered.

I work on both sides of that. On the agent side I try to make the relations among robot, target and environment explicit and causal, so that the structure lives inside a world model and is reflected in the decisions the model supports rather than being re-inferred from correlation. On the human side I work on getting motion out of video and turning it into something that can be recognized, represented and reasoned over. The two meet at the point where a human demonstration stops being a trajectory to copy and becomes a statement of how the world should change.

DIRECTION I

The agent and its environment

01 · INTERACT

How much room a policy should leave is a property of the situation

A clearance margin fixed in advance is wrong in both directions: generous enough for a cluttered room and the robot detours across an empty one; tight enough for an open corridor and it clips a doorway. Adaptive Safety Margins makes the margin a function of what the camera currently sees. A generative policy proposes candidate trajectories and a learnable safety critic, carrying a trajectory-dependent clearance budget, scores and ranks them; geometry is privileged information used offline to teach the critic, so the deployed system runs from RGB-D with no map. What conditions the policy is the interaction, not a reconstruction of the room.

02 · STRUCTURE

Separating what the agent did from what the world did

Under an egocentric camera the robot's own motion and its observations become mutually dependent, and a generic action-conditioned predictor exploits the wrong half of that dependency: it reads target visibility off its own command. The rollouts stay plausible and the semantics go wrong. CST-WM writes the relation among robot, target and environment into the transition itself — target-evidence, robot and observation become separate branches, and the illegitimate edge is deleted architecturally rather than penalized in a loss. Then the leakage is measured, not assumed: a constraint that cannot be shown to hold is not a constraint.

DIRECTION II

Human motion as a record of interaction

03 · EXTRACT

Getting motion out of video, and giving it axes that mean something

Nothing downstream survives a bad signal, and robustness in reading a moving body comes from variation — in viewpoint, in background, in who is performing. SignNet-1M generates that variation rather than going out to collect it: monocular reconstruction recovers body and face, and re-rendering varies viewpoint, scene and identity while preserving the structure that carries the meaning, with every clip tagged by factor and severity. SignDino then asks what axes a motion representation should factor along, and takes the ones the body already provides: left hand, right hand and face are encoded independently over time, with DINO's local–global self-distillation moved off the spatial axis of an image and onto the time axis of one tracked articulator.

04 · REASON

Turning continuous movement into discrete structure

Movement arrives continuously and what we need from it does not. VTaMo makes that correspondence an explicit, differentiable object instead of leaving it implicit inside attention: entropy-regularized optimal transport produces a frame↔token coupling with a learnable null token for whatever matches nothing, and an orthogonal calibration puts the two embedding spaces on a common scale before contrastive learning runs over the reordered features. Prototype-Assisted Causal Translation asks the same question online, where hindsight is not available: a confidence-aware pose representation, a part-specific tokenizer that makes a short window enough to retrieve a pattern, and a strictly causal pipeline that turns the latency–quality trade-off into something stated and measured.

Where the two directions meet: demonstrations that program the world

A human demonstration is normally consumed as a trajectory to imitate. I think it is better read as a specification of how the world should change — an effect program stated over objects, relations and phases rather than over one body's joints. That reading is exactly what joins the two halves of my work: reading motion out of video is how the program is obtained, and a causally structured world model is what turns the program into an execution. The same desired effect admits many executions, and which body performs it should not be part of the specification. I am building toward a world-action model that takes a human demonstration at test time, infers the executions compatible with the effect it describes for a different embodiment, and uses its own learned dynamics to check which of them will actually realize it — in context, with no parameter update and no task-specific robot demonstration.

Learning Adaptive Safety Margins for Visual Navigation

Junyi Hu, Shuaihang Yuan, Geeta Chandra Raju Bethala, Anthony Tzes, Yi Fang

IROS 2026 First author Agent ↔ environment

How much clearance a path needs is a property of the situation, not a constant a designer can fix in advance: set the margin high and the robot detours in open space, set it low and it cuts corners in clutter. We make the margin something the policy reads off the interaction itself. A diffusion policy proposes K = 16 candidate trajectories from egocentric RGB-D, and a learned critic carrying a trajectory-dependent clearance budget scores them. ESDF geometry is privileged information used only offline to supervise the teacher — the deployed selector runs from RGB-D alone, with no map building. 0.783 SR / 0.611 SPL on HM3D against NavDP's 0.710 / 0.529, and 8/10 on the hardest real-world scene with a Unitree G1 trained purely in simulation.

VTaMo: Video–Text Alignment Model for Sign Language Translation

Junyi Hu, Zhewen He, Haomian Huang, Aoxiang Yang, Yi Fang

ECCV 2026 First author Continuous ↔ discrete

Mapping a continuous stream onto a discrete sequence leaves the correspondence between them implicit inside attention, and that correspondence breaks the moment the two stop advancing in the same order. VTaMo makes it an explicit, differentiable object at three granularities: entropy-regularized optimal transport with a learnable null token for local frame↔token coupling, a learnable orthogonal transform over a memory queue for global calibration, and position-aligned contrastive learning over the reordered features. A text-only recovery model then restores fluent sentences from the decoded pseudo-gloss. Nothing in the formulation refers to sign language — it is a matching layer between two sequences of different length and ordering; sign language is where the order mismatch is severe enough that leaving the correspondence implicit stops working.

SignNet-1M: Large-Scale Multilingual Sign Language Video Dataset with Downstream Benchmarks

Zhewen He, Junyi Hu, Haomian Huang, Zhenhua Li, Yu-Shen Liu, Yi Fang

ECCV 2026 Co-author ~1M clips · 2,058 h

A benchmark reports a score but not which factor of variation caused the failure. Existing corpora are shot near-frontal, in studios, with a handful of performers, so models break under viewpoint, background and identity shift with no way to say which one did it. SignNet-1M generates the variation instead of collecting it: ~1M clips across ASL, DGS and CSL from seven source corpora, varied along three structure-preserving axes — 3DGS novel-view rendering, diffusion-based scene editing, and cross-reenactment identity substitution — each tagged with its factor axis and severity. The paired Orig / Zero-shot / Trained protocol then separates a benchmark's coverage blind spot from the training value of the added data; training on SignNet-1M recovers up to +14.71 BLEU-4.

CST-WM: A Causally Structured World Model for Embodied Visual Tracking

Junyi Hu, Shuaihang Yuan, Yi Fang

Preprint First author Causal structure

A camera that moves with the agent makes its own motion and its observations mutually dependent, and a generic action-conditioned predictor exploits the wrong half of that: it writes the current action directly into the state that represents whether the target is still visible. The rollouts stay plausible and the semantics go wrong. CST-WM writes the relation among robot, target and environment into the transition — factorizing the latent into target-evidence, robot and observation branches and deleting that edge architecturally rather than penalizing it. The leakage Jacobian |∂H/∂a| falls from 0.112 to 0.001, and the model that cannot cheat predicts futures worth ranking: +0.12 following rate and +0.15 re-acquisition under long occlusion over a matched baseline that shares the observation interface, planning horizon, candidate budget and action bounds, and differs only in the transition design.

SignDino: Self-Supervised Sign Language Representation Learning via Temporal-Axis Self-Distillation

Junyi Hu, Zhewen He, Haomian Huang, Zhenhua Li, Zhifei Li, Yi Fang

Preprint First author Self-supervised learning

Self-supervised vision learns structure by distilling between two views of one image along the spatial axis. Structured motion has an axis of its own, and the meaningful counterpart of a local crop is not another view of the same frame but a shorter temporal view of the same tracked articulator. SignDino moves the student–teacher recipe onto the time axis: a frozen DINOv3 supplies per-frame embeddings once, and the only thing trained is a lightweight temporal Transformer per anatomical stream — left hand, right hand, face — about 30M parameters in total. Every term in the objective is inherited unchanged from image self-supervision; what changed is the axis the local–global game is played on. Frozen features alone already beat the previous live-fine-tuned state of the art on How2Sign, full fine-tuning reaches 17.9 BLEU-4, and all 16 ASL-Lex phonological features improve — which says the representation learned how articulators are configured and how they move, not how to map anything to English.

Prototype-Assisted Causal Sign Language Translation with Explicit Latency–Quality Trade-offs

Junyi Hu, Zhewen He, Haomian Huang, Zhenhua Li, Yi Fang

Under review First author Streaming recognition

Recognizing motion that carries meaning is one problem offline and a different one online. A streaming system sees noisy pose, cannot wait for hindsight, and is usually judged by a sentence-level score that hides when it committed. Three mechanisms carry the system: a confidence-aware, noise-robust pose representation that stays informative where the underlying keypoint estimates are unreliable; a part-specific VQ tokenizer with discriminative training, so that a short temporal window is enough to retrieve the pattern inside it; and a strictly causal streaming pipeline that constrains which future information a prediction may use. Reporting time-to-first-token, prefix quality and revision rate alongside translation quality turns the latency–quality trade-off into something stated and measured rather than incidental.

Research statement

Interaction is the object; motion is how we get at it

Two directions on one problem

My research studies interaction in the physical world, and it runs in two directions that are more connected than they look. In embodied navigation and tracking, I model explicit relations among the robot, the target and the environment, so that interaction and causal structure can be represented inside a world model and continuously reflected in decision making. In human motion, movement carries how people act on and respond to the world; extracting, recognizing, representing and reasoning over motion is therefore central to understanding those interactions from video.

The common failure in both is the same. A model given the raw stream and a loss will find some function that fits, and the function it finds is usually not the relation you meant. An agent whose camera moves with it can predict whether its target is visible almost perfectly by reading the answer off its own command — plausible rollouts, wrong semantics, and no accuracy metric that notices. A decoder asked to translate a signing video is simultaneously asked to discover a cross-modal reordering it was never told about, and typically learns neither cleanly. In both cases the model fits the data and misrepresents the task. My response has been the same on both sides: state the relation explicitly, put it where the architecture can enforce it, and then build the measurement that would expose it if it had not held.

What building these systems has taught me

Explicit beats latent whenever the relation can be stated. In VTaMo the frame↔token correspondence stops being something a decoder must infer and becomes an object in the model: entropy-regularized optimal transport produces an explicit coupling, with a learnable null token for frames that should match nothing. The alignment can then be inspected, constrained and ablated separately from the translation. The same move on the agent side is Adaptive Safety Margins, where clearance stops being a constant chosen by a designer and becomes a quantity the critic reads off the current interaction.

The right factorization is usually given by the domain, not discovered. A signer is not one blob and an agent is not one state. SignDino encodes the left hand, the right hand and the face independently over time and fuses only downstream; CST-WM splits target evidence from robot state from observation. In both cases the axes were available for free in the problem, and using them cost nothing but bought a representation whose parts mean something.

The dependency you want is rarely the correlation you get. This is the sharpest lesson, and it is why I care about interaction being causal rather than merely predictive. In CST-WM the model can predict target visibility very well by cheating, and no accuracy number distinguishes that from understanding. Deleting the edge architecturally and then measuring that the leakage Jacobian had fallen to zero was the only way to know which model I had.

Sign language is the evaluation, not the design target

Much of the human-motion half gets tested on sign language, and it would be easy to read the work as an application area. It is not built that way, and the distinction is visible in the methods themselves.

SignDino is a modification of DINOv2/v3. Every term in its objective — self-distillation over prototype assignments, masked-token prediction, feature spreading, Gram anchoring — is inherited unchanged from image self-supervision; what I changed is the axis the local–global game is played on, from the spatial extent of an image to the time extent of one tracked stream. Nothing in the loss knows what a sign is. The only sign-specific decision anywhere in the system is which articulators to track, and that is a choice about data preparation, not about method. Its clearest result is not the translation score but the phonological probe: all sixteen sub-lexical features improve, which says the representation has learned how articulators are configured and how they move.

VTaMo has the same shape. Entropy-regularized optimal transport with a learnable null token is a matching layer between two sequences of different length and ordering; the formulation contains no reference to sign language. I have since applied the same alignment mechanism to temporal sub-task segmentation of long-horizon robot-manipulation video, which is the test I actually believe: run a mechanism somewhere it was not designed for.

Sign language is an unusually demanding testbed for reading interaction off human movement: dense, fast, semantically loaded, and badly served by existing benchmarks. That is why I use it. It is not what the methods were built to fit.

How I try to work

A constraint that cannot fail is not a claim. Where I impose structure I try to build the measurement that would expose it if it had not taken hold: the leakage Jacobian and action-swap intervention in CST-WM, the controlled robot-motion-only check that separates selective insensitivity from plain numbness, the paired Orig / Zero-shot / Trained protocol in SignNet-1M that keeps a benchmark's coverage blind spot from being mistaken for the training value of data. Baselines matter for the same reason: the comparison I trust most in CST-WM is against a world model that shares the observation interface, planning horizon, candidate budget and action bounds, and differs only in the transition design.

Where I want to go

The two directions converge on a question I am now working on directly. A human demonstration is normally consumed as a trajectory to imitate, which quietly assumes that the demonstrator's body and the robot's body are interchangeable. They are not, and the assumption is what makes cross-embodiment transfer brittle. I think a demonstration is better read as a specification of how the world should change — an effect program stated over objects, relations, order and phase, deliberately free of whose arm performed it.

That reading turns my two directions into two halves of one system. Reading motion out of video is how the program is obtained; a causally structured world model is what turns the program into an execution. I am building toward a world-action model queried in context: at test time it is given the current scene and a human demonstration of a task for which it has no robot expert data, it infers the executions compatible with the effect the demonstration describes, and it uses its own learned dynamics to verify which of them would actually realize that effect — with no parameter update. Because the same desired effect admits many executions, the model has to produce a distribution over solutions rather than a single mapping, and the demonstration stays in context throughout the episode instead of being collapsed into a goal vector and discarded.

Two harder questions sit behind it, and I would rather work on both than assume someone else will handle the second. The first is whether the structure I currently hand-design can be discovered — the causal factorization in CST-WM and the anatomical streams in SignDino were both mine to choose, which does not scale beyond problems I already understand. The second is what evidence would establish that a discovered structure is real rather than convenient. I expect the evaluation half of that to be as hard as the modelling half.

About

Background

I am an undergraduate in mathematics at Fudan University and a research assistant at New York University, working with Prof. Yi Fang. I am applying for PhD study beginning Fall 2027. Alongside the work above I hold a First-Class Undergraduate Scholarship, run an NSFC undergraduate research project on automated grading of intervertebral disc degeneration with Shanghai Ninth People's Hospital, and work on site at a world-model startup on model research and development.

The topics I have worked on were shaped in large part by the groups I joined. What I take to be my own is the judgement I formed while doing them:

  • A mechanism described as belonging to an application is usually undersold. The honest test is to run it somewhere it was not designed for — which is why VTaMo's alignment layer ended up segmenting long-horizon manipulation video, and why I would rather do that than add another point to a leaderboard I already lead.
  • A result I cannot imagine falsifying is not yet a result. The diagnostic that could have caught me belongs in the paper, not in an appendix — and more than once it has changed what I was entitled to claim.
  • When the structure of an interaction can be stated, stating it beats hoping a larger model recovers it from correlation. Scale is not a substitute for knowing what your representation is supposed to mean, and a model that fits the data can still misrepresent the task.

My training is in mathematics, and it shows in the tools I reach for: optimal transport as a differentiable matching layer, factorizations chosen for what they forbid rather than what they permit, constraints written into an architecture instead of asked for in a loss. It also shows in what I find unsatisfying — a number that improved without an account of why.

Outside the papers I build most of my own infrastructure: multi-GPU training, simulation environments, and the real-robot side of deployment on Unitree G1, xArm7 and Franka FR3 platforms. I find that the gap between a method that works in a table and one that works on a robot is where most of the honest information is.
